## 6. Discussion

**6.1 What the evidence supports.** Across five settings the size of the random-split inflation tracked how identifiable the groups were from the features. In simulation, optimism was about zero when groups could not be recognised from features (fingerprint 0, optimism about -0.003 for all models, 8 datasets) and rose to 0.135 (logistic regression), 0.240 (random forest) and 0.219 (gradient boosting) with strong group fingerprints. In 738 E. coli genomes, random splits overstated AUC in all 36 model, metric, grouping and label combinations, by 0.013 to 0.084, and same-group nearest-neighbour rates sat far above chance (for example 0.27 against 0.025 for sequence type). In the Scottish soil survey the effect was small and inconsistent (median 0.002 to 0.012; the interval excluded zero in 13 of 45 combinations). In 5,836 E. coli genomes with gene presence/absence features (Section 5.3), random splits scored higher in all 30 combinations (median 0.096, range 0.043 to 0.297), with near-perfect group recognisability. In 3,619 Salmonella enterica genomes from the same study (Section 5.4), random splits again scored higher in all 30 combinations, by more (median 0.171, range 0.066 to 0.444).

**6.2 Why the soil result differs (partly supported, mechanism untested).** The same-group nearest-neighbour rate gives a measured reason: in the soil survey the excess of the rate over chance was small (at most about 0.10 across the three groupings), whereas in the E. coli data it was 0.20 to 0.31 and in the simulation with strong fingerprints 0.82 (Fig 6). Groups that are hard to recognise from the features leave little for a model to exploit under a random split, which is consistent with the small soil optimism. This is a descriptive pattern, not a test: each dataset has only a few distinct identifiability values, cells within a dataset share them and are not independent, and the relationship is not monotonic across settings (E. coli has higher excess identifiability than the mid-level simulation but lower optimism, so identifiability alone does not predict the size of the effect). Identifiability is also not sufficient: in the Yu et al. E. coli data the excess rate was 0.72 to 0.93, similar to or above the strong-fingerprint simulation (0.82), yet the median optimism (0.096) was below the simulated 0.135 to 0.240, and values within that dataset ranged from 0.043 to 0.297 depending on label, grouping and model. The Salmonella data (excess rate 0.83 to 0.88, median optimism 0.171) agree with this: identifiability is high, the effect is large, but the size still varies by a factor of seven across labels, groupings and models (0.066 to 0.444). Other explanations remain open: soil labels (detection of one gene from the others) may be dominated by within-sample co-occurrence; the 20 km sampling grid may leave neighbouring sites too far apart for spatial autocorrelation to matter; and 199 samples give wide intervals.

**6.3 What the audit is for.** The audit does not say whether a model is good. It says how much the reported score depends on the split. A paper reporting only a random-split score on grouped data leaves that dependence unknown; SplitAudit makes it a number the authors and readers can see, and the reporting checklist lists what to state.

**6.4 What we saw in the literature, and its limit.** In the small coded sample, split type and handling of near-duplicate samples were often not stated, and one paper filtered features on the full dataset before splitting. This is five papers, two read in full; it shows that such gaps occur, not how often. We make no claim about the field's prevalence of leakage.

**6.5 Practical guidance (modest).** (i) Declare the groups. (ii) Report random and group-held-out scores on the same data and model. (iii) Report an identifiability diagnostic with its chance level. (iv) Check for features that duplicate the label. Items (i), (ii) and (iv) are cheap; our own first E. coli run showed the last matters: duplicate gene naming schemes leaked the label (AUC about 0.99) until we removed both.

## 7. Limitations

1. **Four real datasets: three bacterial genome sets from two studies and one soil survey.** Results may not transfer to other organisms, labels or sampling designs. The third dataset used a feature filter tightened for compute, and only the E. coli files of that study were used.
2. **Uncertainty is understated.** Real-data intervals reflect repeated splits of one dataset, not sampling variability across datasets. Multi-dataset intervals in simulation were wider.
3. **Optimism is not correctness.** Linked genes (plasmids, operons, clonal lineages) can make part of the predictability genuine, not leakage. Also, groupings here are nested or overlapping (sequence type within phylogroup; country partly confounded with sampling effort), so a held-out group may still resemble training groups, and results are per grouping, not additive.
4. **Soil analysis design.** Labels were gene detections chosen by a mechanical rule (prevalence nearest 0.5), not an applied prediction task; the table had no environmental covariates; spatial blocks came from k-means and the block count changed the result; same-operon genes were not removed; one row with an impossible latitude was dropped.
5. **Default models.** No hyperparameter tuning; three model families only.
6. **Literature evidence is thin.** Two papers read in full (from fetch summaries, to be re-verified against the PDFs), one abstract-only, two unread; one coder, no agreement estimate.
7. **Simulation is built to leak.** Simulated optimism (up to 0.24) is a designed effect and should not be read as an estimate for real data (real-data maximum 0.444, against 0.084 in the first E. coli table).
8. **Checklist untested.** It has been applied to two fully assessed papers only, by the authors.

## 8. Conclusion (draft)

Random cross-validation can overstate performance on grouped data, but how much depends on whether the groups are identifiable from the features. SplitAudit measures this directly with an optimism score, a model-free identifiability diagnostic and a permutation null. In simulation the effect is large when groups leave a recognisable signature and absent when they do not; in E. coli genomes it was consistently positive, modest in one table (0.013 to 0.084 AUC) and larger with gene-content features (0.043 to 0.297 in E. coli, 0.066 to 0.444 in Salmonella, 60 of 60 cells); in a Scottish soil survey it was small and inconsistent. We therefore recommend not a blanket rule but a routine check, reported with the groups declared and the interval's meaning stated. Next steps are validation on more datasets, a systematic review of how environmental AMR studies split their data, and testing the checklist with independent coders.
